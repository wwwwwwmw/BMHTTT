Abstract 
Despite all the technical approaches to monitoring threats and detecting incidents, manual incident reporting is crit- 
ical at all organizational levels of cybersecurity. However, in its current state, reporting suffers from challenges such 
as underreporting, lack of reporting channels, and uncertainty about what should be reported. The phenomenon of 
incident reporting itself is not clearly defined and occurs in different facets, from reporting phishing emails to the 
IT department to reporting vulnerabilities to national authorities. This makes it difficult to design effective socio- 
technical incident-reporting systems (IRS) according to overarching principles. This review article addresses these 
challenges by drawing on insights from the field of safety, where IRS are well-established. We find that a broad range 
of events is reported, various reporting channels on different organizational levels exist, and key design factors of 
successful IRS have emerged. Based on these lessons from safety, we propose a taxonomy for cybersecurity re- 
porting that includes noncritical events, such as near misses, and latent factors, such as weak security controls. We 
suggest that also in cybersecurity new reporting channels can be established, e.g. for reporting of noncritical events 
to nonpunitive supra-organizational bodies or for employee reporting. When designing IRS for cybersecurity, factors 
such as case-based learning, voluntariness, impunity, independence, and feedback should be taken into account in 
order to encourage reporting. 
Keywords: cybersecurity; incident reporting; vulnerability reporting; safety science 
Introduction 
Since the introduction of IT security functions in the 1980s, organiza- 
tions have relied on incident reporting—initially focusing on internal 
events, such as employee computer misuse [ 1 ]. In the late 1980s, the 
increasing interconnectedness of organizations through the Internet 
led to a rise of external events, such as the distribution of malware 
and the need for cross-organizational computer incident response 
teams (CSIRTs) to prevent incidents affecting multiple organizations 
[ 2 ]. These teams relied on manual incident reporting. Today, some or- 
ganizations not only voluntary report incidents but are even obliged 
to report serious ones [ 3 ]. Thus, despite all technological innovations 
to automatically detect and report incidents, the human ability to rec- 
ognize and report incidents remains central to cybersecurity [ 4 , 5 ]. 
However, manual incident reporting is fraught with numerous 
challenges. A fundamental issue lies in the lack of a unified under- 
standing of what incident reporting entails. It encompasses various 
forms, such as phishing reports by employees, the reporting of data 
breaches to national CSIRTs, or, in a broader sense, vulnerability re- 
porting [ 6–8 ]. The ambiguity surrounding what qualifies as an inci- 
dent often leads to uncertainty about what should be reported [ 3 ].

Reporting obligations are fractured across countries [ 9 ]. Further- 
more, structured reporting channels are frequently absent, compli- 
cating the reporting process [ 10 ]. And even when reporting occurs, 
its effectiveness is not guaranteed: a dual problem of underreport- 
ing relevant incidents and overreporting irrelevant ones is commonly 
observed [ 4 , 11 ]. Additionally, the absence of follow-up actions to 
the reports often renders them ineffective, as they fail to translate 
into appropriate measures [ 11 , 12 ]. This overall deficit in clarity and 
structure hinders the development of effective reporting systems and 
the optimal use of human capabilities to detect and report incidents 
[ 13 ]. 
This is where the safety discipline offers solutions. Established 
ideas and concepts from the field of safety are already being adapted 
for cybersecurity (e.g. cyber-resilience systems [ 14 ]). Researchers 
have started to systematically translate safety knowledge to the cy- 
bersecurity domain [ 15 , 16 ]. The main rationale for the transfer is 
that many problems and solutions are similar. For example, while 
cyberattacks are carried out intentionally, unlike accidents, the need 
for organizations to protect themselves is similar [ 17 , 18 ]. However, 
safety has a decades-long experience advantage in the development 
of holistic prevention concepts that do not only involve isolated tech- 
nical aspects but the organization as a whole [ 16 ]. 
Also in safety, incident reporting is considered a cornerstone of all 
safety measures—especially in high-risk industries [ 19–21 ]. Since the 
1970s, incident reporting systems (IRS) have been implemented to 
enable individuals and organizations to report incidents, fostering a 
culture of safety and continuous improvement [ 22 , 23 ]. Evidence sug- 
gests that incident reporting can foster organizational learning and 
ameliorate safety (e.g. in aviation [ 24 ], occupational safety [ 25 ], or 
patient safety [ 26 ]). In the aviation sector alone, NASA’s voluntary 
Aviation Safety Reporting System (ASRS) has led to numerous safety 
improvements. For example, reports of distracting conversations be- 
tween cockpit personnel during critical phases of a flight and conse- 
quential incidents led to the “Sterile Cockpit Rule”of the American 
air traffic control, which limits conversations to necessary coordina- 
tion during taxi, takeoff, and landing [ 27 ]. Therefore, learning not 
only from severe but also from seemingly minor reports is seen as 
key to preparing for, mitigating, and preventing future events [ 23 ]. 
To achieve these goals, it was always necessary to look for ways to 
obtain human support, for example, through nurses reporting inci- 
dents in hospitals [ 28 ]. 
If we adapt the successful practices from safety and avoid mis- 
takes of the past again, we can substantially improve reporting in 
cybersecurity, given its pivotal function [ 29–31 ]. Therefore, in this 
article, we review the literature on IRS in safety and assess the appli- 
cability of its insights to cybersecurity. Our guiding question is: 
How are effective IRS designed for cybersecurity? 
We first consider the purpose of incident reporting in both fields. Sec- 
ond, we propose a taxonomy to categorize what can be reported 
and describe areas we regard as currently neglected in cybersecu- 
rity. Third, we compare IRS on different organizational levels and 
show opportunities for new reporting channels. Fourth, based on a 
systematic review of previous literature, we outline key design con- 
siderations for IRS and their relevance for cybersecurity. Lastly, we 
discuss our findings and shed light on implications for researchers 
and practitioners. 
Why report? The purpose of IRS 
What is known as an IRS in safety is a socio-technical system facil- 
itating the structured reporting, collation, and analysis of incidents 
[ 22 ]. IRS are employed locally in organizations but also on an in- 
dustry or national and international level [ 22 ]. Initially, IRS were im- 
plemented in sectors such as aviation, chemical production, marine 
transportation, military, nuclear power production, railway trans- 
portation, and healthcare to increase safety through a better under- 
standing of accidents and their causes [ 23 p. 14]. Central to this were 
systems for mandatory reporting of accidents to authorities for in- 
vestigation, such as airplane accidents [ 32 ]. The scope of these sys- 
tems was later broadened to include voluntary reporting of incidents 
considered precursors to accidents [ 21 , 33 ]. An example is ASRS, 
launched in 1976 as an industry-wide system to gather, examine, and 
address aviation near misses (e.g. a near-aircraft collision) to mini- 
mize the risk of actual aviation accidents [ 34 ]. Voluntary reporting 
has evolved to become integral in enhancing safety [ 23 ]. 
Thus, a central question for the design of an IRS is what function 
it has (i.e. what purpose it serves), because the form and function 
of such a socio-technical system are closely intertwined [ 35 ]. Imple- 
mented by both the private and public sectors, the overall purpose of 
IRS is to institutionalize the improvement of safety in organizations. 
The following goals have been identified [ 23 p. 22, 1168, 35 p. 33, 
36 p. 201, 37]: 
r Learning from accidents and less severe events to improve safety 
by determining hazards, investigating the causes of incidents, 
finding patterns and trends, and identifying improvement mea- 
sures. 
r Assessing safety-related risks and cost reduction measures. 
r Caring for victims of incidents, compensating victims, and 
demonstrating goodwill. 
r Cultivating a safety culture within an organization by fostering a 
positive attitude and raising awareness. 
Mandatory IRS additionally serve the following goal [ 36 ]: 
r Complying with regulatory requirements for reporting, investi- 
gation, and record-keeping. 
Also in cybersecurity, incident reporting has been recognized to 
enhance cybersecurity of organizations, industrial sectors, or even na- 
tions [ 37 ]. Unlike in safety, there is no single notion for the various 
reporting phenomena such as IRS [on closer inspection also safety 
uses different notions (e.g. near miss reporting systems), but we be- 
lieve that the phenomenon of IRS is nevertheless more clearly de- 
fined.]. Instead, reporting exists in various forms, for example as part 
CSIRTs [ 2 , 38 , 39 ], as coordinated vulnerability disclosure [ 40 ], or 
employee phishing reporting [ 8 ]. Regarding the function, not only 
voluntary reporting but also mandatory reporting systems have been 
employed. For example, the Network and Information Security Di- 
rectives (NIS/NIS2) in the European Union and the Cyber Incident 
Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act (CIRCIA) in the USA require 
critical infrastructure organizations to report critical incidents [ 3 , 6 , 
41 ]. Similarly, privacy legislation such as the EU General Data Pro- 
tection Regulation (GDPR) obliges organizations to report breaches 
[ 41 ]. 
Consequently, we can say that the purpose of IRS in cybersecu- 
rity is very similar to that in safety: learning from incidents, risk as- 
sessment, promoting a security culture, fulfilling legal requirements, 
or informing those affected (e.g. in the event of a data breach) [ 38 , 
39 , 42 ]. The consequences of cybersecurity incidents, such as busi- 
ness interruption or loss of data are often less tangible compared 
to the safety-related consequences, e.g. physical or psychological ad- 
verse impacts, for people (e.g. [ 20 ]). However, cyber incidents can 
also cause severe harm to individuals—for example, if cyber–physical 
systems, such as a pacemaker or an electric grid are hacked [ 43 ].

Given the many parallels, the question arises as to why the find- 
ings on safety reporting have not been systematically used for cy- 
ber security. One explanation might be that cybersecurity is still very 
monodisciplinary and silo-oriented, with a lack of understanding 
nontechnical aspects and consideration of other disciplines [ 44 , 45 ]. 
In contrast, safety has grown to a highly interdisciplinary field which 
recognizes that IRS can only be successful if they are understood as 
part of a greater socio-technical system [ 46 , 47 ]. 
Other reasons for the absence of knowledge transfer may be ter- 
minological confusion, a lack of abstraction of safety knowledge, or 
the (assumed) specificity of the cybersecurity context. For example, 
as discussed before, there is no comprehensive notion of IRS and re- 
lated terms in cybersecurity despite various forms of de facto incident 
reporting. However, if cybersecurity views each type of reporting as 
unique and independent of one another, there will be no search for 
overarching solutions. 
To overcome the differences between the disciplines, we will first 
discuss the reporting terminology before looking at different cate- 
gories of reporting systems. In doing so, we will realize that certain 
forms of reporting do not yet exist in cybersecurity (e.g. near miss 
reporting). 
What can we report? A look at what causes 
incidents 
To understand what can theoretically be reported, it is instructive to 
look at what causes incidents. The discipline of safety has developed 
numerous types of “accident causation models”[ 48 ]. In the popular 
“Swiss Cheese Model” (The same type of thinking is often implic- 
itly and explicitly implied in cybersecurity concepts such as the cyber 
kill chain, MITRE attack, depth-in-defense, or root cause analysis 
[ 16 , 49 ].) a hazard may lead to an accident despite numerous lay- 
ers of defense . An accident may occur if flaws (holes) caused by la- 
tent factors and adverse events in each layer of defense align [ 50 ]. 
Adverse events range from minor incidents (with no significant ad- 
verse impact) and near misses (with potentially significant adverse 
impact) to actual accidents (with a significant adverse impact) (See 
appendix for original safety definitions.). In safety IRS, all of these 
entities are reported. Reporting of hazards ranges from external haz- 
ards, such as “dangerous weather conditions in aviation” to inter- 
nally created hazards such as “a bucket of slippery oil at a retail 
store” [ 51 ]. Latent factors are reported such as flight crew fatigue 
[ 52 ] or a lack of training. Finally, not only types of adverse events 
are reported but also their harm is reported (i.e. the consequences 
of an adverse event), such as the “negative side-effects of a medica- 
tion”[ 53 ]. 
As shown in Fig. 1 , the Swiss Cheese Model can be directly trans- 
lated to cybersecurity using NIST’s terminology [ 54 , 55 ]. Due to the 
importance of the actor, we distinguish between threat actor and 
threat source (analogous to NIST SP 800–150), unlike safety, which 
only recognizes the hazard. A threat actor (e.g. attacker, malicious 
insider) and threat source (e.g. intention to exploit a common vul- 
nerability, negligent user behavior) may initiate a threat event ( i.e. 
marginal incident , near miss or critical incident ), which exploits vul- 
nerabilities and predisposing conditions (latent factors) in layers of 
security controls (Referred to as “security control effectiveness” in 
SP 800–137 [ 56 ].) (e.g. firewalls, network segmentation, processes, 
and awareness training). Critical incidents lead to significant adverse 
impact . 
In cybersecurity IRS, each of the entities of the Swiss Cheese 
Model can be reported, too (see reporting taxonomy in Table 1 ). Re- 
porting “a group of hackers exploiting a specific software vulnera- 
bility”to alert potential victim organizations [ 57 ] is an example of a 
threat actor (hackers) and threat source (intentional exploitation of 
a vulnerability). Within an organization also malicious insiders can 
be reported, for example, by colleagues [ 58 , 59 ]. 
In the reporting of threat events , the distinction between critical 
incidents, near misses, and marginal incidents is typically not made 
[ 60 ]. While reporting of critical incidents and their impact (e.g. large 
financial loss or interruption of business) is often required by law, 
there is potential to report near misses and marginal incidents. Al- 
though proposed over 20 years ago and discussed in the research 
community now and then [ 29–31 , 61 ], near miss reporting has only 
recently found its way into practice {e.g. [Article 6(5) NIS 2 Direc- 
tive], [ 62 ]}. Reporting near misses could provide more frequent learn- 
ing opportunities than critical incidents alone [ 63 ]. Individuals and 
organizations may be also less likely to withhold or strategically re- 
port in their own self-interest, given the lower importance and like- 
lihood of negative consequences of reporting (e.g. no regulatory or 
civil liability) (Tamuz, 1988, p. 1ff.). 
Latent factors in cybersecurity are either vulnerabilities (e.g. tech- 
nical vulnerabilities, insufficient processes, lack of training, fatigue of 
SOC staff, and missing controls) or predisposing conditions (e.g. pri-

How should we report? Different types of 
reporting systems 
Having addressed the questions of why to report and what can 
theoretically be reported, the next question is how to report. 
We therefore compare different reporting systems including their 
channels [ 23 ]. 
IRS in safety have been established at the local level, allowing re- 
porting to organizations by staff or external reporters. For example, 
in many hospitals, nurses and doctors are called upon to report inci- 
dents in organizational reporting systems [ 28 ]. Also staff in nuclear 
power plants is required to report safety issues [ 68 ]. 
Comparable structures on the organizational level can be identi- 
fied in cybersecurity. Internal reporters can be security profession- 
als or regular employees (Fig. 2 , 1). For example, security teams

reason for direct reporting to the IRS is that organizations may be 
unresponsive to internal reports or unwilling to share information 
with external authorities. 
In cybersecurity, these higher-level IRS correspond to sectoral 
and national CSIRTs, supervisory authorities, and cyber insurance 
providers. Recently cybersecurity, privacy, and financial legislations 
require organizations to report incidents to supervisory authorities 
[ 6 , 41 , 75 ]. Voluntary reporting is also possible, for example to 
sector-specific CSIRTs [ 76 ]. These higher-level IRS receive reports 
based on specific thresholds from organizations (e.g. severity of a 
data breach) (Fig. 2 , 3). Reports can also be submitted by individ- 
ual external reporters (e.g. security researchers) (Fig. 2 , 5). IRS can 
then notify individual organizations, for example, about new vul-

reporting of threats, near misses, vulnerabilities, and other security- 
relevant events to higher-level IRS is in its infancy [ 3 ]. Systems such 
as ASRS, in which employees report wrongdoing within organiza- 
tions to higher-level IRS are also just evolving (Fig. 2 , 4). At present, 
cybersecurity whistleblowing and dedicated reporting infrastructure 
are rare; Singapore’s national cybersecurity whistle-blowing system 
is one of the few notable examples [ 78 ]. 
To conclude, there is potential in cybersecurity for more system- 
atic reporting of a wider range of security observations, both to the 
IRS in the organization and to higher-level IRS. Both internal and 
external reporters should be supported with improved reporting 
channels (e.g. employee reporting beyond phishing). The reporting 
of noncritical incidents to sectoral or national CSIRTs still has room 
for improvement. In sectors with high security requirements, such as 
aviation, an IRS for whistleblowing similar to the ASRS for security 
could even be introduced. 
How should we design reporting? Critical design 
themes 
So far, we have established that IRS in safety and cybersecurity 
serve comparable purposes. By looking through the lens of the Swiss 
Cheese Model, we highlighted the potential to include noncritical in- 
cidents in cybersecurity reporting. Furthermore, we observed that, 
relative to safety, cybersecurity reporting could benefit from a more 
systematic approach both within organizations and with higher-level 
institutions. In this section, we will address the general design factors 
that need to be considered when introducing IRS in an organization. 
We do this by identifying relevant themes in the safety literature and 
discussing them in the context of cybersecurity. 
Methodology 
To identify critical design themes, we performed a systematic review 
following a concept-centric approach [ 79 ]. Due to the immense num- 
ber of publications on the design of IRS in the safety literature, we 
limited our search to review articles (“review of reviews”). Figure 3 
depicts the flow of information through the different phases of the 
review. We did not set a date range, and we considered English- 
language literature in peer-reviewed journals and conferences as well 
as English-language books. We used the string “(reporting system 
OR reporting program) AND (incident OR adverse event OR error 
OR near miss OR sentinel event OR safety accident OR confiden- 
tial OR close call OR near accident OR accident precursor OR pre- 
cursor)” to search in title, abstract and keywords in the databases

Case-based learning 
The main objective of an IRS should be to learn from individual 
cases—opposed to using it as a statistical tool or to monitor safety 
performance [ 34 , 81 , 82 ]. These individual cases allow for the iden- 
tification of risk factors and the determination of the need for fur- 
ther investigation [ 81 ]. Ideally, reports contain information-rich nar- 
ratives [ 83 ]. While traditional learning has focused on what to learn 
from a case, more recent approaches focus on how to learn and un- 
derstand learning as an organizational process [ 26 , 84 ]. 
Learning from individual incidents—as suggested in safety—is 
still considered in its infancy in cybersecurity [ 85–87 ], as the broader 
context of the incident is barely considered (e.g. learning processes) 
[ 42 , 88 ]. The fact that information about attacks are often considered 
private and confidential, for example, to prevent additional attack- 
ers from learning about an uncovered vulnerability, hinders learn- 
ing (e.g. via case studies), especially between organizations [ 89 ]. Yet, 
organizations would highly benefit from an exchange about uncov- 
ered vulnerabilities that might affect systems or processes across or- 
ganizations. Instead, information is reduced to statistics with some- 
times questionable informative value [ 90 ]. Notwithstanding, there 
are also good examples of in-depth analysis following a cyber in- 
cident, such as the investigation in the wake of Log4j [ 91 ]. How- 
ever, there is still potential for a better understanding of the causes 
of cyber incidents and the question of how learning processes are 
designed. 
Competence 
In safety, potential reporters (e.g. nurses, pilots, and factory work- 
ers) need to know that reporting is possible, what the purpose of 
reporting is, and what can be reported [ 92–94 ]. This cannot simply 
be assumed. Expertise is also required to be able to assess whether an 
incident has occurred (as prerequisite to reporting it) and to analyse it 
[ 82 ]. For example, in the ASRS, reports are independently judged by 
two experienced analysts (e.g. retired pilots or air traffic controllers) 
to ensure intersubjective verifiability [ 95 ]. Furthermore, the incident 
investigators’ multidisciplinary competencies have also been empha- 
sized [ 83 , 84 , 96 ]. In conclusion, staff training is essential to build 
competences [ 93 ].

Confidentiality 
In safety, most systems allow confidential reporting, and some even 
anonymous reporting [ 102 , 83 ]. Confidentiality or anonymity for the 
reporter and the content reported may be the only way a reporter will 
provide insight. However, due to the lack of contextual information, 
anonymous reporting makes it more difficult to investigate an inci- 
dent [ 23 ]. A compromise is to anonymize the reports after receiving 
them [ 102 ]. 
Confidentiality is even more crucial in cybersecurity to avoid shar- 
ing knowledge with attackers and motivate further attack [ 89 , 103 ]. 
The high need for protection against threat actors might explain why 
reporting and information sharing is challenging. Also the circle of 
recipients of reports and the criticality of the information must be 
defined very precisely (e.g. with classification standards) [ 89 , 104 ]. 
Few reporting systems allow anonymous reporting [ 105 ]. Apart from 
losing contextual information, this may exempt reporters from legal 
obligations, and lead to noncompliance with policies [ 106 ]. However, 
in some cases this may be the only way to collect reports [ 73 , 78 ]. 
In summary, confidentiality—and probably anonymity—are central 
characteristics of reporting in cybersecurity, too. 
Ease of use 
In early instances of safety reporting systems, reports were made on 
paper and only later electronically. In both cases, however, the ease of 
use is crucial and lowers the access barrier since reporting incidents 
is not an everyday event for many people [ 107 , 102 ]. As the main 
objective of the IRS is the analysis of the reported incident by trained 
personnel, very little input should be required from the reporter (e.g. 
a narrative description of the incident). Making reporting too compli- 
cated by eliciting potential causes is deemed of secondary importance 
to simply recording the event [ 108 ]. Low-threshold reporting, which 
favors simplicity, is therefore central to an IRS. 
Cyber incident reporting is typically not a daily routine for re- 
porters. Sometimes, they do not even know how, to whom to report, 
and what to report [ 73 , 87 ]. Phishing reporting illustrates the impor- 
tance of making the reporting process straightforward—for example, 
through reporting buttons in email clients [ 8 , 109 ]. This principle 
similarly applies to employees’ reporting of cybersecurity incidents

Feedback 
In safety, feedback to reporters has been crucial as it helps to make 
learning from incidents a participatory practice [ 81 , 92 ]. Feedback 
also creates shared accountability between reporters and reporting 
system operators, reinforcing its effectiveness. It is not only the spe- 
cialists who should see analysis outcomes; employees who report in- 
cidents should be able to gain insights into outcomes to be able to 
recognize the value of their contribution [ 112 , 113 ]. When possible, 
feedback is also shared with other parties for more generic learning 
(e.g. other departments, government agencies) [ 95 ]. In summary, a 
lack of feedback prevents learning, making organizations more sus- 
ceptible to life-threatening accidents and lawsuits. Therefore, feed- 
back within an IRS is central to turning isolated learning into orga- 
nizational learning. 
One could argue that feedback is even more important in cyber- 
security, as many incidents occur as anomalies (e.g. high traffic on 
webservers) without the inherent feedback often associated with per- 
sonal safety (e.g. emergency braking) [ 31 ]. However, reporters are not 
always acknowledged for their actions, nor are they notified about 
outcomes [ 114 ]. For example, literature suggests that also security re- 
searchers who identify vulnerabilities often do not receive feedback 
either [ 12 , 40 ]. In short, appreciative and informative feedback can 
reinforce learning and reporting in cybersecurity [ 8 , 109 ]. Stronger 
communication skills are needed to provide useful feedback [ 101 ]. 
This might be especially relevant for the security department given 
that cybersecurity-related roles, such as that of the CISO are often 
alienated within their organizations [ 115 ] and that relationships and 
communication flows between employees and IT security staff are 
often dysfunctional [ 116 ]. 
Impunity 
Fear of sanctions or repercussions is a common barrier to reporting 
[ 117 , 118 ], and anticipation of legal consequences means not all in- 
cidents are reported [ 119 ] or the report might be biased in favor of 
those involved. Such a negative association may reduce the number 
of reported incidents. In response, some systems protect the reporter 
not only through anonymity. For example, in the case of ASRS in 
the US aviation sector, reporters are granted partial immunity by the 
Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) in some circumstances [ 34 ]. 
In cybersecurity, impunity for individual reporters is also crucial 
[ 31 , 106 ]. Protecting and even praising reporters—rather than sham- 
ing them—could encourage reporting and learning [ 61 , 120–122 ]. 
Some organizations have already adopted “blameless postmortems”
in the aftermath of incidents, which focus on identifying the incident’s 
causes [ 123 ]. Others have introduced a hall of fame for reporters 
[ 124 ]. Some jurisdictions have implemented whistleblower protec- 
tion laws whose safeguards also extend to cybersecurity-related dis- 
closures (e.g. EU Directive 2019/1937). Future potential lies in dedi- 
cated cybersecurity reporting infrastructures. In order to create an en- 
vironment that provides “psychological safety” [ 125 ] for reporters, 
attitudes toward IT security staff and their relationships with re- 
porters need to be improved in many organizations [ 116 ]. 
At the level of reporting by organizations, fear of consequences 
is also an important reason for reluctance to report serious inci- 
dents [ 87 ]. Consequently, some laws regard the self-reporting of in- 
cidents as a mitigating factor when determining fines (e.g. Article 83 
of GDPR [ 126 ]). 
Independence 
In safety, IRS should be as independent as possible from individuals 
or organizations with conflicting interests or authority over the re- 
porter [ 127 ]. For example, a direct line manager prevent unwelcome 
news from being passed up the hierarchy [ 81 , 127 ]. Instead, reports 
could be made to an independent team that directly reports to senior 
management [ 81 ]. Here, national/sectoral IRS can offer a certain de- 
gree of independence. For example, near misses are reported via ASRS 
operated by NASA, which has no regulatory function or enforcement 
authority. NASA is therefore considered a trusted party [ 34 ]. Inde- 
pendent “learning bodies”similar to the ASRS in the aviation sector 
can also be found in other sectors, such as rail and maritime indus- 
tries or healthcare [ 127 ]. 
The independence of reporting is critical to cybersecurity as well. 
For example, there is a risk that the reporter will be punished for non- 
culpable or nonserious errors (e.g. vulnerabilities disclosure) [ 120 ]. 
However, a look at the national level shows that in current cyberse- 
curity and privacy reporting systems, incidents are typically reported 
to supervisory authorities that have sanctioning authority (e.g. NIS 
2, GDPR) [ 3 , 6 , 41 ]. Therefore, it is necessary to adapt laws to be able 
to report noncritical incidents without potential legal consequences 
to independent entities. 
Integration 
In safety, IRS were often assumed to be sufficient as the sole tool for 
achieving organizational safety [ 82 ]. However, IRS should only be 
understood as one measure of an overarching safety system. Accord- 
ingly, they should be linked to other measures (e.g. risk management 
and wider safety programs) [ 26 , 93 ]. For example, data from an IRS 
can be combined with observational studies (e.g. a survey among or- 
ganizations) or automatic monitoring of incidents [ 23 ]. There is also 
a need to integrate the IRS with other safety practices (e.g. daily rou- 
tines) and the cultural frame of those who report [ 112 ]. 
In cybersecurity, IRS can be understood as an integral part of cy- 
ber risk-management measures. In organizations, this integration is a 
significant challenge [ 88 ]. However, some organizations use reports 
to identify risks and prioritize investments (e.g. in training or infras- 
tructure). Furthermore, at a national/sectoral level, IRS can be com- 
plemented with incident surveys to obtain an even more complete and 
less biased picture [ 128 ]. In general, IRS must also be seen as comple- 
mentary to informal alternatives, such as trust groups with high levels 
of confidentiality (e.g. Ops-Trust, NSP-Sec, and FIRST). An IRS in se- 
curity should, therefore, also be considered a central building block 
within a more extensive security management system.

success or failure of reporting actions [ 83 ]. At best, management acts 
as a role model for safety, creates a blame-free environment, pro- 
vides resources to train employees, allows time to report incidents, 
and helps resolve goal conflicts. At worst, management introduces

Shared ownership and accountability 
Shared ownership and accountability within the whole organization 
are necessary to promote reporting and ensure that learning occurs 
at all levels, including individual employees, teams, and the safety de- 
partment [ 34 , 127 ]. The idea of shared ownership to encourage re- 
porting has also been called a “just culture”[ 50 ]. The reporter should 
feel responsible for security and reporting incidents but, at the same 
time, not automatically be blamed for all lapses [ 50 ]. To promote 
shared ownership, it has been suggested that the IRS be organized 
as locally and decentralized as possible instead of being a centralized 
and hierarchical system [ 26 ]. For example, in a hospital it could be 
established at each unit rather than at a central quality management 
department. 
When implementing incident reporting in cybersecurity, owner- 
ship and accountability are crucial but also challenging. Unlike per- 
sonal safety, the consequences of a cyber risk may not always affect 
the reporter directly, making it difficult to encourage ownership [ 31 , 
135 ]. Reporters might not realize that their reports are relevant to the 
entire organization’s security [ 135–137 ] nor that they are responsi- 
ble for reporting [ 138 ]. Nevertheless, some approaches try to pro- 
mote ownership and accountability by making cybersecurity part of 
a business strategy relevant for the whole organization or through 
a supportive organizational culture and end-user engagement (e.g. in 
the development of security policies) [ 139 , 140 ]. In summary, incident 
reporting in cybersecurity faces the challenge of balancing shared 
ownership and shared accountability while maintaining clear respon- 
sibilities. 
Speed and adaptivity 
Reports should be investigated quickly, and the results fed back to the 
reporter for immediate action [ 107 , 127 ]. This can help reduce the 
loss of information (e.g. observations made by those directly involved 
in the incident) [ 36 ]. At the same time, the system should allow a 
certain degree of adaptivity [ 83 ], such as what constitutes an incident 
that changes over time [ 84 ]. 
Owing to ever-changing attacks and broad attack surfaces, speed 
and adaptivity are even more critical in cybersecurity as seen in events 
such as the 2021 Microsoft Exchange Server vulnerability [ 141 ]. 
However, there is a risk that existing reporting and handling pro- 
cesses will be overburdened by too many irrelevant reports and alerts 
[ 4 , 114 ]. To enable a timely response to reports, it is essential to 
triage them into potentially security-relevant and irrelevant reports 
[ 142–144 ]. In the future, machine learning could be used even more 
to distinguish relevant from irrelevant reports, and large language 
models could help quickly extract structured information from nar- 
rative reports [ 145 ]. 
Standardization 
Standardization is required to ensure the efficiency of IRS. Standard- 
ization includes a common understanding between reporters and ex- 
perts about what incidents are—and when and how to report them 
[ 36 , 146 ]. A common language provided by taxonomies, in addition 
to free-text narratives, can preserve the richness of narrative reports 
while enabling systematic organization and analysis of reported data 
[ 92 , 107 , 147 ]. However, too much standardization can also nega- 
tively affect the system. For example, asking for too much detailed 
information from reporters instead of short narratives can discour- 
age reporting [ 81 ]. Accordingly, researchers call for a “pragmatic”
and “flexible”standardization of reporting systems [ 81 ]. 
In cybersecurity, a common understanding among reporters and 
investigators of what cyber incidents are, when to report them, and 
how to process reports is equally necessary [ 3 , 148 , 149 ]. Various 
standards already exist that describe what can be reported, how 
serious reported issues are (e.g. CVSS), and what can be learned 
from incidents [ 142 , 150 ]. There are different requirements for stan- 
dardization of reporting for laypersons compared to experts, as 
well as reporting within organizations or to other entities (e.g. su- 
pervisory authorities). For some target groups, standards do not 
yet exist. For example, if organizations want to implement report- 
ing systems for their employees, new standards have to be de- 
veloped. However, similar to the safety scenario, reporting must 
not be too structured, or it will discourage reporters from coming 
forward [ 8 ]. 
System orientation 
Safety science suggests that IRS should be based on a sufficiently com- 
plex understanding of the causes of incidents [ 127 , 113 ]. In the early 
days of safety, elementary incident causation models were applied 
(e.g. human error, technical malfunction), and only over time were 
more complex incident causation models developed (e.g. the Swiss 
Cheese Model, STAMP) [ 151 ]. Critics therefore suggest that reports 
should not only be used to correct obvious errors, but also to consider 
changes to the overall safety management system [ 26 ]. 
In cybersecurity, the simplistic understanding of incidents and 
their causes has been criticized because instead of fostering in-depth 
learning, these superficial changes tend to result in the mere fixing of 
obvious causes [ 88 ]. However, more recently, a systemic understand- 
ing of cyber incidents that considers structural causes is becoming 
prevalent [ 16 , 152 ]. This allows us to understand why certain types 
of reports recur and to adopt holistic packages of measures that ad- 
dress technical, human, and organizational factors [ 16 ]. That is why 
more people should work in cybersecurity in the future who have 
been trained in systemic thinking [ 101 ].

Mandatory and voluntary reporting approaches have specific benefits 
and limitations [ 83 ]. Sometimes, an obligation to report can improve 
the effectiveness of an IRS [ 107 ]. Mandatory reporting might also 
be required by law to investigate critical incidents (e.g. with casual- 
ties) or to address the issue of underreporting. However, mandatory 
reporting creates new problems, such as biased information, infor- 
mation loss, or overreporting of irrelevant incidents [ 23 ]. For this

cybersecurity 
The human ability to detect and report incidents is central to main- 
taining and improving cybersecurity [ 4 , 5 ]. Despite numerous tech- 
nological advances, such as the use of AI in threat detection, cyber- 
security cannot do without human factors such as experience and 
intuition when it comes to recognizing threats and possible ways to 
mitigate them [ 5 , 13 , 158 ]. However, it is unclear how an effective 
IRS can be designed for cybersecurity. This starts with the fact that 
there is no clear terminology around reporting, that different forms of

We addressed these challenges by drawing upon decades of ex-

to other approaches, for example, an empirical evaluation of cyber- 
security reporting policy [ 87 ]. Safety is the discipline that has similar 
problems and approaches to solutions, but is highly interdisciplinary 
[ 46 ] and has decades of experience with the design of reporting sys- 
tems on all organizational levels [ 23 ]. Notably, many mistakes in 
dealing with reporting systems have already been made. In the past, 
IRS have been hailed as the solution to all safety problems and later 
perceived as filing cabinets of irrelevant reports [ 81 ]. Finally, a realis- 
tic perspective has emerged and the systems have become established 
with a wealth of experience in their (mis)design. 
Therefore, safety knowledge offers solutions to strengthen cyber- 
security reporting and improve incident information, which is cur- 
rently often sparse and unreliable [ 90 , 153 , 159 ]. Many individuals 
and organizations hesitate to report incidents [ 87 ]. While other dis- 
ciplines, such as organizational science, information systems, or psy- 
chology could also help to design IRS, we believe that the priority 
should initially be on safety science. Only in this discipline has the 
IRS phenomenon, which is identical to cybersecurity, already been 
studied to its full extent. 
With growing cyber threats and the increasing importance and 
maturity of incident reporting in cybersecurity, now is the right time 
to leverage interdisciplinary experience of the sister discipline. 
Our findings and their contribution to cybersecurity can be sum- 
marized as follows: 
Finding #1: various existing reporting mechanisms can be 
grouped under the umbrella of IRS, a concept well established in 
safety. The objective of an IRS is to uphold and improve cybersecurity 
by systematically learning from reports made on different organiza- 
tional levels [ 23 ]. 
Solution #1: the advantage of this abstract perspective is that IRS 
are not seen as isolated and independent phenomena, but as instances 
of a similar socio-technical system. For example, mechanisms ensur- 
ing timely feedback in IRS are equally important for employees that 
report phishing mails as for external parties that report vulnerabili- 
ties [ 12 , 114 ]. If these are equally understood as IRS of an organiza- 
tion, care can be taken to ensure that efficient feedback mechanisms 
are implemented in both cases. 
Finding #2: using the Swiss Cheese Model to systematically clas- 
sify what can be reported in IRS, we proposed a reporting taxon- 
omy and found potential in reporting noncritical events. For exam- 
ple, near misses are rarely reported to national CSIRTs [ 3 , 29 ]. 
Solution #2: the proposed taxonomy helps to significantly in- 
crease the number of events from which to learn, beyond learning 
from critical incidents that represent rare learning opportunities [ 63 ]. 
Indeed, the first companies have begun to define near misses for their 
context, such as a prevented malware execution, and started to track 
them [ 62 ]. 
Finding #3: in both safety and cybersecurity, local IRS in orga- 
nizations and higher-level IRS have been implemented. For example, 
individual organizations can maintain CSIRTs while at the same time 
participate in higher-level CSIRTs that span multiple organizations in 
the same sector. In cybersecurity, not all reporting channels are exist- 
ing or elaborated. 
Solution #3: internal and external reporters should be supported 
with improved reporting channels (e.g. employee reporting beyond 
phishing) [ 12 , 71 , 73 ]. Reporting also noncritical incidents to sec- 
toral or national CSIRTs still has room for improvement [ 3 ]. Even 
whistleblowing systems like the ASRS could be implemented in sec- 
tors, where security is very important. Similar to reporting noncritical 
events, the expansion of reporting channels creates additional learn- 
ing opportunities [ 63 ]. 
Finding #4: various factors are relevant for the design of effec- 
tive IRS. For example, it is recommended that reporting should be 
voluntary wherever possible, feedback should be provided and that 
appropriate trust-building mechanisms such as confidentiality, im- 
punity, and independence should be anchored in the system [ 34 , 81 ]. 
Automatization and additional IT staff is required to follow up on 
reports in a timely manner. 
Solution #4: these factors should be considered when improving 
existing and designing new IRS. For example, mandatory reporting 
systems for noncritical incidents to nonindependent bodies—as re- 
cently proposed in connection with the NIS 2 Directive [ 3 ]—might 
be ineffective as reporters may fear negative consequences. Legisla- 
tion should study the case of ASRS, which only became successful 
when it was moved from FAA to the independent NASA [ 34 ]. 
Strengths and limitations 
This review integrates principles from safety science into cybersecu- 
rity, presenting a novel interdisciplinary perspective. Through a com- 
prehensive literature review and thematic analysis, we lay a robust 
foundation for our findings. Overall, we address significant gaps in 
cybersecurity practices, proposing scientifically grounded solutions 
to improve reporting efficacy and learning opportunities, which can 
directly be applied in industry. 
However, the review has also several limitations. Theoretical 
knowledge transfer from safety cannot replace original evidence of 
what makes IRS effective in cybersecurity. However, we believe it still 
offers valuable starting hypotheses due to strong parallels. In addi- 
tion, subjectivity is inevitable in the selection, interpretation, and syn- 
thesis of the vast safety literature, especially since some areas, such 
as aviation incident reporting, are underrepresented because we ex- 
cluded gray literature (e.g. NASA technical reports). Despite these 
limitations, leveraging decades of safety-related experience to im- 
prove incident reporting in cybersecurity remains a worthwhile en- 
deavor. 
Avenues for future work 
Numerous follow-up questions arise for practitioners and re- 
searchers. These begin with the precise definition of terms. For exam- 
ple, organizations need to agree on what constitutes a near miss in 
cybersecurity. We have provided several examples, however these def- 
initions are highly context specific. From the perspective of a highly 
regulated financial institution, a near miss may be defined differently 
than from the perspective of a hospitality organization. 
It is also conceivable that the taxonomy can be further refined 
to be better tailored to organizations. For example, the Human Fac- 
tors Analysis and Classification System, which is based on the Swiss 
Cheese Model, distinguishes between factors at different organiza- 
tional levels that promote an incident [ 160 ]. Poor management or 
insufficient resources, for example, can be latent accident factors just 
as much as individual employee behavior. This would also make it 
easier to identify weaknesses within the organization. 
Furthermore, some reports of noncritical events and latent fac- 
tors might qualify as “leading indicators” that predict critical inci- 
dents while others might not. For example, no evidence was found 
in the safety domain that noncritical events are generally good pre- 
dictors of critical events [ 36 , 161 ]. These correlations should also be 
understood in cybersecurity to prioritize reports, derived measures,

Other questions relate to the identified design factors. For

if not all noncritical incidents might leading indicators for critical 
incidents they still could be used to improve risk management. 
Conclusion 
Leveraging insights from safety science, this article emphasizes the 
potential of IRS to transform cybersecurity practices. Our findings 
suggest that different forms of incident reporting should be seen as 
variations of the same phenomenon. This perspective provides op- 
portunities for new approaches, such as near-miss reporting and sets 
requirements for how the reporting systems should be designed: re- 
porting should be voluntary, confidential, and easy, with reporters 
protected from negative consequences. Investigations must be timely 
and focused on holistically learning from individual cases, and re- 
porters should receive feedback. 
IRS should rely on standards, be integrated with other security 
measures, and remain independent of entities with the power to dis- 
cipline reporters. Management should raise awareness of the IRS and 
actively support its implementation. Reporters and investigators re- 
quire relevant knowledge and skills for effective reporting and in- 
vestigation. Lastly, ownership and accountability should be shared 
between reporters and the receiving organization to ensure partici- 
pative learning.
